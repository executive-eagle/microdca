What is bitcoin?

Digital money that no government or bank controls, with a fixed supply of 21 million coins.

You don't have to buy a whole one. Bitcoin splits into 100,000,000 tiny pieces called "sats", so $10 buys a small slice.

Its price swings a lot. It has fallen more than 50% several times, and it has also grown many times over in the long run. That's why people often buy a little at a time instead of all at once.

Try buying a little every week

Buying a little at a time

Invest the same dollar amount on a schedule, no matter what the price is.

This is called dollar cost averaging (DCA). Say you buy $25 of bitcoin every week. When the price is low, your $25 buys more. When it's high, it buys less. Over time you end up paying an average price, and you never have to guess the perfect moment to buy.

It won't stop you from losing money if prices fall and stay down. And if prices only go up, putting everything in on day one would have done better. Its real strength is that it's easy to stick with.

Dividends and payouts

Cash some investments pay you just for owning them.

Payouts are paid per share. If a share pays 10% a year on $100, you get $10 a year for each share, split into monthly, quarterly or even daily payments.

The yearly payout divided by the price you pay is called the yield. "Yield on cost" is your yearly income divided by the money you put in. Payouts aren't promised: companies can lower them, skip them or stop them.

Preferred stock (STRC, SATA, STRF, STRK, STRD)

A share that pays a set dividend and gets paid before regular stock, but after lenders.

Strategy (the company formerly called MicroStrategy) and Strive sell preferred stock and use the money to buy bitcoin. Each share has a $100 "stated amount" the dividend is based on, but its market price can be above or below $100.

Cumulative means a skipped payment is still owed later (STRF, STRK). Non-cumulative means a skipped payment is gone for good (STRD). These shares are not backed by the company's bitcoin. If the company runs into trouble, payouts can stop and prices can fall.

Strategy's shareholders vote on Oct 28, 2026 on paying dividends every day instead of monthly or quarterly. MicroDCA's "Daily (proposed)" setting shows what that would look like.

Covered-call ETFs (BTCI)

Funds that give up some future growth in exchange for cash today.

An ETF is a basket of investments you can buy like one stock. A covered-call ETF sells other investors the right to buy its holdings at a set price, collects a fee for that, and pays the fee out to you every month. That's how BTCI pays such a high rate.

The catch: when bitcoin jumps, these funds don't keep all of the gain, and over time their price has tended to drift down. A big payout rate isn't the same as making money. Always look at what the whole investment is worth, not just the income.

Compounding

Reinvesting your payouts so your payouts start earning payouts.

Put $1,000 into something paying 10% a year. Take the payouts as cash and after 10 years you've collected $1,000 on top of your $1,000, so $2,000 total. Reinvest every payout and you'd have about $2,594, because each year's payout is a little bigger than the last.

The catch: compounding only helps while the investment keeps paying and its price holds up.

Margin (borrowing to invest)

Borrowing money from your broker, using your investments as the guarantee.

Margin makes gains bigger and losses bigger, and you pay interest the whole time. If prices drop too far, the broker can sell your investments for you at the worst moment (a "margin call").

Paying down a margin loan is like earning a guaranteed return equal to the interest rate. MicroDCA's payout splitter (Advanced mode) lets you send part of your income to paying it off.

Income so you're never forced to sell your bitcoin

The goal isn't to beat bitcoin. It's to never have to sell it at a bad time.

Most people who hold bitcoin eventually sell some, to pay for life, an emergency or a big purchase. And the moment you need cash is often the moment prices are down. Selling then locks in the loss, and in the U.S. it can also trigger a tax bill.

The idea is to build a second engine next to your bitcoin:

  1. Set aside part of what you earn and buy investments that pay you every month (or every day).
  2. Let that income cover your needs, or reinvest it into more income funds, some bitcoin, or paying down debt.
  3. If income isn't enough, borrow a little against your holdings at a reasonable rate instead of selling, and pay it back from the income.
  4. Worst case, sell the income funds first. Your bitcoin is the last thing you ever touch.

Done well, you keep your original bitcoin and own a growing pile of income-producing assets you can live from or borrow against.

What critics get right

"Just buy more bitcoin." In a strong bull market, money in income funds will grow far less than the same money in bitcoin. That's real. But it compares the wrong things. The fair comparison isn't "income fund vs. bitcoin", it's "income fund vs. selling bitcoin when you need money."

Borrowing: small is the whole trick

Borrowing works when the loan is small next to what it's borrowed against. If you borrow 20% of your portfolio's value and everything drops 30%, your loan is now about 29% of what's left: uncomfortable, but survivable. Borrow 50% and the same drop puts you near 71%, where a broker can sell your investments for you. That's exactly the forced selling you're trying to avoid. Brokers often require extra cushion for volatile holdings, and interest keeps adding up, so plan to pay loans down from income.

Taxes: "return of capital"

Strategy says all of its 2025 preferred dividends were a return of capital, and it expects that to continue because it has no taxable earnings. Many covered-call ETFs report much of their payout the same way.

Return of capital isn't taxed when you receive it. Instead it lowers your cost basis (what the IRS considers you paid). Once your basis reaches zero, further payouts are taxed as capital gains, usually at lower long-term rates if you've held more than a year. When you eventually sell, the lower basis means a bigger gain. So it's mostly tax deferral, not tax-free, and you or your broker need to track your basis.

It isn't guaranteed. Strategy's treatment depends on it continuing to have no earnings for tax purposes, and a fund's mix can change year to year. For a covered-call ETF, return of capital can also simply mean you're getting your own money back while the share price falls. This is general information for US residents, not tax advice. More on taxes

Rules of thumb people use

  • Size the income to cover a real expense first (a bill, then rent), not to chase the highest yield.
  • Favor payers that kept paying through a crash, and spread across issuers. Not everything should depend on one company.
  • Keep any borrowing small, and keep some plain cash for emergencies.
  • Judge every holding by total return (price change plus payouts), not yield alone.

Taxes (for US residents)

You mostly pay tax when you sell at a profit or receive income. Just holding isn't taxed.

This is for people who pay US taxes. Other countries have very different rules, so check your local rules if you live elsewhere. Not tax advice.

Selling

For taxes, bitcoin is treated as property. Selling it, or spending it, at a profit is a taxable gain. Same for selling a stock or ETF.

  • Held a year or less: short-term gain, taxed like your paycheck (10% to 37%).
  • Held more than a year: long-term gain, taxed at 0%, 15% or 20% depending on your income. For most people that's 15%.
  • Selling at a loss can lower the tax on your other gains.

When you buy a little at a time, each purchase has its own clock. Some of your bitcoin can be long-term while the newest buys are still short-term.

Payouts

Regular dividends are taxed in the year you get them: "qualified" dividends at the lower long-term rates, "ordinary" dividends like regular income.

Return of capital (ROC) is different. The issuer is saying the payout is your own money coming back, not profit. It isn't taxed when you receive it. Instead it lowers your cost basis (what the IRS treats you as having paid). When you sell, the lower basis means a bigger taxable gain, usually at long-term rates. If your basis reaches zero, further ROC payouts are taxed as capital gains right away.

Strategy says all of its 2025 preferred dividends were return of capital, and Strive says the same for SATA. NEOS estimated about 92% of BTCI's 2026 payouts as return of capital. These are estimates and can change every year.

Where to find the real numbers

  • 19a-1 notices: funds and ETFs (like BTCI) post these on their website with each payout, estimating how much is income vs. return of capital. Always check the fund's own website for the most accurate, latest notice.
  • Form 8937 and press releases: companies like Strategy post these when a payout changes your cost basis.
  • Form 1099-DIV: the final word. Your broker sends it after the year ends, with the official split.

Borrowing

Borrowing against your investments isn't a sale, so it isn't taxed. Interest on money you borrow to spend usually isn't deductible. Interest on money borrowed to buy investments sometimes can be. Ask a tax professional.

Questions

Is this real money?
No. Everything on MicroDCA is a simulation. Nothing is bought or sold, and you can't lose or make money here.
Where do the numbers come from?
Real daily closing prices and real past dividend payments from Yahoo Finance. Prices refresh every 15 seconds while the stock market is open, and bitcoin's price comes live from Coinbase.
Is this financial advice?
No. It's a learning tool. Past results don't predict the future. Talk to a licensed professional before investing.
Can I invest through MicroDCA?
No. To actually buy any of these, you'd use a brokerage account. Some brokers let you buy fractions of a share or schedule recurring buys; others don't, especially for preferred stock.
What about taxes?
MicroDCA ignores taxes and fees. Strategy has said its recent dividends were treated as "return of capital", which is taxed differently than normal income. A tax professional can explain what that means for you.
What's the difference between Simple and Advanced?
Simple shows the essentials in plain English. Advanced shows every setting: exact dates, custom splits, adding your own tickers, sending payouts to margin or bills, and more detailed stats.
